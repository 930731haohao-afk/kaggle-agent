  \vfill
  {\raggedright\small
   Flags \textbf{exactly} the competitions that need outside data.\quad
   \textbf{Every} controlled arm beat its baseline.\par
   \vspace{1.2mm}
   {\color{mut}No local signal I tested picks the form.}\par}
  \vspace{2mm}

  \note{The conventional answer is downstream: extra ideas among the candidates
    you blend. I built that, measured it: worth about ten to the minus five. So
    this architecture injects upstream. Before any modelling, a dossier reads
    the problem statement --- does this task need outside data --- and emits
    whitelisted, leakage-guarded joins. The rest is conventional: the language
    model decides at every stage, gradient boosting fits, tree search is the
    loop. In a blind sweep it flagged exactly the competitions that need it and
    stayed silent on the rest, and every
    controlled arms beat their baselines on the real leaderboard. Nothing local I tested
    tells me which form to use. (37s)}
\end{frame}

        \foreach \i in {0,...,59} {

          \else

              (\cc*4.7,-\rr*4.7) rectangle ++(4,-4);
          \fi}
      \end{tikzpicture}\\[2mm]
      {\small 60 duels: {\color{barblue}\textbf{38 decided}},
       22 undecidable --- not ties.\par}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \vfill
  {\small\color{mut}Verdict: the ordering replicates on both halves,
   \emph{and} the gap exceeds its own movement.\par}
  \vspace{2mm}

  \note{Those are my own numbers, and between agents local validation cannot
    arbitrate: one lane scored six point seven out of fold, forty-nine on the
    leaderboard. So every verdict is a real submission. Kaggle scores each
    submission on two halves of the test set. My first threshold used one
    agent's drift between halves and called most duels undecidable ---
    but that drift is shared: it moves all three agents together. Take the gap
    between two agents on both halves and the common drift cancels. A verdict
    needs both halves to agree, and the gap to beat its own movement. Sixty
    duels: thirty-eight decided; twenty-two are not ties, but readings the test
    refuses to make. (45s)}
\end{frame}

% ============================================================= 4. THE READING
\begin{frame}{What the frozen three-way run measured}
  \vspace{0.5mm}
  {\small\color{mut}20 competitions \quad$\cdot$\quad one architecture, frozen
   before the run \quad$\cdot$\quad no my-agent lane could reach a leaderboard\par}
  \vfill
  \ratebar{my-agent}{barblue}{0.667}{16--8}{66.7\%}
  \ratebar{NVIDIA}{mut}{0.423}{11--15}{42.3\%}
  \ratebar{AIDE}{mut}{0.423}{11--15}{42.3\%}
  \vfill
  {\small Wins--losses over each agent's own decided duels: 24 for my-agent,
   26 for each yardstick (38 decided duels $=$ 76 agent-side outcomes).\par
   \vspace{1.2mm}
   Best private score: \textbf{10 of 20} (NVIDIA 7, AIDE 3).\par}
  \vspace{2mm}

  \note{Here is the reading. Twenty competitions, three agents, one architecture
    frozen before the run, and no my-agent lane could reach a leaderboard. The two
    yardsticks are frozen too: AIDE searches from zero, the NVIDIA agent
    reproduces the highest-voted public kernel. I fixed nothing in either.
    Mine wins sixteen of its twenty-four decided duels, sixty-six point seven
    percent, against forty-two point three for each. That number measures the
    whole architecture; the injection decision is measured separately, by the
    controlled arms. (38s)}
\end{frame}

  \note{Where it loses is the interesting half. My two worst losses are the
    only two competitions with a mature public solution, and there the
    reproduction agent beats me decisively --- twenty-eight against five and

    strong public solution exists, search wins where none does. The standing is
    also fragile: drop one competition and it reads seventy-two point seven, and one
    duel sits exactly on its noise floor. A ranking belongs
    to the method and the evaluation set together --- mine included. (37s)}
