AI Agents for Data Visualization and Kaggle Competitions

An end-to-end LLM agent (my-agent) benchmarked against two frozen yardsticks on 20 Kaggle competitions — with the apparatus needed to make the comparison honest
Author: Wei-Hao Huang (黃威昊)  ·  School: National Chengchi University, Department of Economics
Advisor: Dr. Tso-Jung Yen (顏佐榕), Institute of Statistical Science, Academia Sinica  ·  Summer Internship 2026 · Report v4 (2026-08-17)

1 · Motivation

Why these tasks are hard

  • Kaggle competitions demand the full expert loop — validation design, feature engineering, model selection, ensembling — where the difference between top entries sits at the 4th–5th decimal of the metric.
  • Local cross-validation is an unreliable compass: it contradicts the real leaderboard in 9 of 13 comparable competitions we measured.
  • Data visualization (EDA, diagnostics, reporting) is essential but repetitive: every dataset needs distribution, drift, and correlation views before modeling can be trusted.

Why an AI agent

  • Tireless, systematic search: 22–80 candidate configurations per competition, every experiment logged and reproducible.
  • Runs the same disciplined protocol on 20 competitions serially — a human cannot hold this consistency.
  • Generates per-dataset EDA figures and per-competition reports automatically, so every claim is grounded in an artifact.

2 · Workflow

An LLM reasons at every stage; Auto-ML libraries are invoked through the shell; a cross-competition experience library supplies evidence-backed priors; a tree search over candidate configurations is the optimisation loop.

Six-stage pipeline: problem dossier → EDA → features → modeling → evaluation → submission. The v5 dossier layer classifies the task and injects external data upstream, before any modeling.
Tree-search loop: ensembles are first-class nodes with OOF caching and weight search; exploit until plateau, then one forced exploration burst.

4 · Advantages & Difficulties

Expected → experienced

  • Expected: the agent would quickly beat most humans. Experienced: median percentile 72.3 — solidly above average, with wins concentrated where no mature public solutions exist.
  • Expected: one run and one score decide a winner. Experienced: score noise + run-to-run variance leave 27% of duels undecidable; a paired error model became mandatory.
  • Expected: the agent follows the rules by default. Experienced: a transcript audit caught one lane reading its own competition's recorded findings — and the clean re-run scored worse, proving the contamination advantage was real.
  • Fairness is expensive: isolated data roots, credential-free sandboxes, and per-lane transcript audits cost real engineering time — but without them the numbers are not comparable.

5 · Discussion — advice

  • State the error model with every claim. A ranking without an error scale is noise: 27% of our duels are undecidable, and the winner flips when the evaluation set changes.
  • Never trust local CV for between-agent claims. Score on the real leaderboard; CV describes a pipeline's internal view only.
  • Freeze your yardsticks. Improving a baseline mid-study silently turns the benchmark into an optimisation of the baseline.
  • Isolate mechanically, then audit the transcript. Sandboxes stop leaks you anticipated; reading what the agent actually did catches the ones you didn't — in both directions (we hit one real contamination and one auditor false positive).
  • Put knowledge injection upstream. Injected at the blend stage it was worth ~10⁻⁵; moved into problem identification it lifted the canonical failure case from last to first.

Where knowledge enters

  • Downstream (blend stage): measured gain ~10⁻⁵ — abandoned.
  • Upstream (problem dossier): fires on exactly the 2/18 competitions that need external data; all 6 of 6 controlled external-data arms beat their matched baselines.
  • But no local signal can select the form of injection — the one surviving selector died on its first pre-registered prediction.
