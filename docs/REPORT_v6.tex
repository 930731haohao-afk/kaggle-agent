\begin{center}\textbf{\large Abstract}\end{center}
\noindent We present \textbf{my-agent}, an end-to-end agent that runs a machine-learning competition from raw data to submitted prediction with no person in the loop: an LLM reasons and decides at every stage --- problem identification, exploratory analysis, feature engineering, modelling, evaluation, submission --- while gradient-boosting libraries are invoked through the shell and a tree search over complete candidate solutions serves as the optimisation loop. We benchmark it against two frozen, methodologically distinct reference agents --- \textbf{AIDE} (LLM tree search) and the \textbf{NVIDIA Kaggle Agent} (public-kernel reproduction) --- on 20 Kaggle competitions, plus three mid-complexity competitions spanning language, physiological time series and medical imaging. Because an agent's own cross-validation cannot arbitrate between agents, every verdict is a real submission scored on the competition's live leaderboard, and every comparison is adjudicated by an error model read off Kaggle's own two-way split of each test set. \textbf{my-agent leads the standing, winning 16 of its 24 decided duels ($66.7\%$) against $42.3\%$ for each reference agent}, taking the best private score on 10 of the 20 competitions and beating its own development-lane score on 13 of 20; on the three mid-complexity competitions it takes the three-way best on all three. The same error model refuses to call 22 of the 60 duels at all, and the agent's two worst results fall on the two competitions with a mature public solution, where faithful reproduction beats autonomous search. One architectural finding follows from the same measurements: knowledge injected downstream, among the models being blended, was worth about ${\sim}10^{-5}$, so this architecture injects it upstream at problem identification instead --- and no local signal we tested can select the \emph{form} that injection should take.

\section{Introduction}

Machine-learning competitions are \emph{scorable tasks}: a submission receives a single measurable quality score on a hidden test set, and a public leaderboard records how it compares against thousands of human teams. Solving them end-to-end has traditionally demanded expert labor and extensive trial and error. LLM-driven coding agents have recently begun to automate this loop: AIDE performs LLM-guided tree search over candidate solutions~\cite{aide2025}; ERA couples an LLM rewriter with tree search and externally injected research ideas, outperforming strong baselines across 16 Kaggle Playground competitions~\cite{aygun2026}; and industrial agents skip search altogether by reproducing the strongest public solution to a given competition~\cite{nvidia2026}.

This report is about one such agent, \textbf{my-agent}, built over the course of this internship: a hybrid design in which an LLM reasons at every stage of the competition pipeline, Auto-ML libraries are invoked through the shell for what they do best, a cross-competition experience library supplies evidence-backed priors, and a tree search over candidate configurations serves as the optimisation loop (Figures~\ref{fig:arch} and~\ref{fig:tree}). It also documents itself: 22 five-section ML specification reports for my-agent and 17 re-documenting the AIDE runs are attached (Appendix~\ref{app:specs}).

What the agent is worth is an empirical question, and this report answers it by measurement rather than by argument: 20 Kaggle competitions, two frozen reference agents, every verdict a real submission scored on the live leaderboard. my-agent leads that standing, winning 16 of its 24 decided duels ($66.7\%$) against $42.3\%$ for each reference agent, and takes the best private score on 10 of the 20. The same measurement refuses to call 22 of the 60 duels at all, and it locates the agent's two worst results precisely: both fall on competitions with a mature public solution, where faithful reproduction beats autonomous search.

One design decision inside the agent earned its own experiments, because our first version got it wrong. Knowledge injected downstream, among the models being blended, measured about ${\sim}10^{-5}$; the architecture therefore injects it upstream, at problem identification, through a stage that classifies task family, train--test window relation, split policy and external-data need before any model is fitted, emitting typed operators into a whitelisted, leakage-guarded external-data module.

All results are stated against two frozen, methodologically distinct yardsticks: \textbf{AIDE}, LLM tree search with no cross-competition memory, and the \textbf{NVIDIA Kaggle Agent}, which reproduces the highest-voted public kernel. All three run on 20 Kaggle competitions plus three mid-complexity competitions; because local estimates cannot be trusted to arbitrate between agents, every verdict among the 20 is scored on the real leaderboard via late submission, while the three mid-complexity competitions are graded offline and adjudicated under the error model of Section~\ref{sec:apparatus}. my-agent's benchmark lanes run under the frozen architecture in sandboxes (Section~\ref{sec:fairness}); all 20 are complete, audit-adjudicated and scored by a separate credentialed operator step, so the benchmark tables carry real my-agent leaderboard scores and paired verdicts throughout (Sections~\ref{sec:exp1} and~\ref{sec:winrates}).

\section{Methods}

\subsection{The Agent}

The method is implemented as a Claude Code Skill (\texttt{kaggle-agent}) around a division of labor: the LLM (Claude Code) handles problem understanding, validation-strategy design, creative feature engineering, result interpretation and the choice of next step, while LightGBM / XGBoost / CatBoost / Optuna, invoked via the shell, handle model selection, hyperparameter optimization and stacking/ensembling. Execution follows the pipeline of Figure~\ref{fig:arch}, whose \textbf{Stage 0.5} problem dossier decides what kind of problem the task is before any modeling (Section~\ref{sec:v5}) and is evaluated in its own right in Sections~\ref{sec:exp5v5} and~\ref{sec:exp6v5}. Modeling and evaluation run in one of two optimization modes:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Linear iteration protocol}: propose a hypothesis, run the experiment, record the result --- used for the first pass and for small competitions.
  \item \textbf{Tree search (preferred)}: once the linear protocol has produced a baseline single model and at least one blend, the agent switches to tree search. Internal evaluation shows tree search winning 9 of 10 competitions with 1 tie and 0 losses.
\end{itemize}

Three cross-cutting mechanisms support the pipeline (Figure~\ref{fig:arch}):

\paragraph{Cross-competition experience library} (\path{knowledge/experience.md}): every insight must carry an evidence field (\texttt{competition, exp \#N, score A $\to$ score B}); entries without a score delta are rejected. The agent queries the library before EDA and before modeling, and writes validated new insights back after experiments. This is the most fundamental design difference between my-agent and the two reference methods.

\paragraph{Experiment records and external data.} Every experiment's parameters, features, split scheme, and scores are logged in structured form (\path{experiments.json}, \path{experiments_tree_v3.json}), making every number in this report traceable; the external-data layer realizes the dossier's typed operators as whitelisted, leakage-guarded joins raced against the no-external baseline inside the search (Section~\ref{sec:v5}); and each lane records a structured EDA pass (distributions, drift, correlation) before modelling, which the skill's reporting layer renders, with the dossier and experiment records, into a per-competition ML specification report whose every printed number must trace to a logged experiment (Appendix~\ref{app:specs}).

\subsection{The Injection Layer}
\label{sec:v5}

The purple elements of Figures~\ref{fig:arch} and~\ref{fig:tree} form a single layer, and its placement carries the design argument. An earlier downstream variant injected external ideas at the blend stage and measured ${\sim}10^{-5}$; the architecture moves the injection point upstream to problem identification, where the question of what kind of problem this is gets decided.

\paragraph{Stage 0.5 --- problem dossier.} Before EDA, the agent answers ``what kind of problem is this?'': task family, train--test window relation, split policy, and external-data need, written to a structured \path{dossier.json}. Judgments are grounded in \path{knowledge/task_priors.md}, a library of task-level priors each carrying an evidence field from our own benchmark. Reading competition-specific discussions or kernels is forbidden --- the dossier must derive from the problem statement plus task-\emph{type} knowledge only, keeping the method distinct from reproduction-based agents. The dossier is a prior, not a conclusion: EDA must verify its hypotheses and record a verdict.

\paragraph{External-data module.} A whitelist of public sources, joined through leakage-guarded as-of merges: an external value may reach a prediction row only if dated at or before the row's period minus a declared lag. The module was adversarially verified, and the findings it reproduced --- including silent-future-leak classes --- were fixed under regression tests.

\paragraph{A typed contract between the two layers.} The first version of this stack failed
instructively: the dossier proposed World Bank GDP for s3e19 \emph{as a country-level scale
covariate}, but the execution layer could express exactly one transformation --- appending a
column to the feature list --- and since a gradient-boosted tree cannot extrapolate a feature
beyond its training range, the semantics evaporated between the layers with nothing raising an
error. Both layers now read one closed vocabulary of typed operators
(\path{knowledge/injection_operators.md}), and the dispatcher writes a coverage ledger, so a
capability the execution layer cannot realize leaves a trace instead of resembling success
(Appendix~\ref{app:coverage}).

\subsection{The Two Yardsticks}

\textbf{AIDE} (open-source agent, gen-1) performs LLM-driven tree search: at each step it writes a complete solution, executes it, reads the error or score, and mutates accordingly. It carries no cross-competition memory --- every competition starts from zero.

\textbf{NVIDIA Kaggle Agent} (skill) reproduces the strongest public kernel: it researches the competition context, selects the highest-\emph{voted} solution-type kernel, ports it faithfully, and retrains on local data.

Both reference methods are frozen --- no improvements of any kind, even where we found weaknesses (the NVIDIA skill even ships a score-based selection tool we deliberately left disabled) --- since improving a yardstick mid-study would make earlier and later data incomparable and would quietly turn the benchmark into an optimization of the reference methods.

\section{Measurement Methodology}
\label{sec:apparatus}

Two pieces of apparatus underlie every comparison below: a fairness and execution-discipline
protocol that makes a score attributable to a method rather than to an accident of the shared
machine, and an error model that says which score differences are readable at all. The
offline-graded mid-complexity competitions of Section~\ref{sec:exp7run2} are the one exception
to the error model's reach.

\subsection{Fairness Protocol}
\label{sec:fairness}

The three agents share one machine, so four isolation conditions are mechanically enforced
rather than left to convention (Table~\ref{tab:fairness}).

A fifth rule governs the experimenter rather than the agents: when the environment is
defective, fix the environment, never hint to the affected side.

\paragraph{Benchmark execution discipline.} For my-agent's 20 benchmark lanes the protocol is
tightened further. A benchmark manifest (\path{docs/rerun_manifest.json}) fixes exactly one
workspace, one canonical evaluator, and one search driver per competition; each lane executes in
an isolated root, holds \emph{no credentials} (the Kaggle token is stripped from the
environment, so a lane can neither consult the leaderboard nor submit), and sees only its own
workspace. A \emph{per-lane transcript audit} then reads every tool call the lane made, and the
launcher halts the whole queue after two consecutive audit failures, on the reasoning that two
in a row indicts the pipeline rather than one lane.

\paragraph{One version per table.} An architecture change invalidates every lane that ran before
it, so the benchmark is versioned rather than patched: the run root is built once, its frozen
file surface recorded in a manifest whose own hash is stored \emph{outside} the root where no
lane can reach it, and a clean-slate gate checks both before the queue starts. When the agent
changed --- most consequentially when the external-data layer was added --- the in-flight run
was archived and the benchmark restarted from a freshly built, freshly hashed root. Seven such
partly-finished runs are archived beside the accepted one; no table in this report mixes lanes
from two versions.

\subsection{The Paired Test}
\label{sec:exp2}

\paragraph{Why an error model is needed at all.} In the development-lane leaderboard
measurements this apparatus was built on, several top-two gaps sat at the fourth or fifth
decimal place (s5e10: $0.00003$; s6e2: $0.00000$); at that scale a report that declares winners
without an error model is reporting its own noise back to itself.

\paragraph{How --- the error scale comes from the data itself, not from assumptions.} Kaggle randomly splits each test set into two disjoint subsets, public and private, and one submission receives one score on each. These are two independent estimates of the same model, so their difference is that competition's metric sampling noise.

\paragraph{A failed first attempt, worth recording.} We first used a single agent's own public--private gap as the threshold, and 8 of 10 competitions came out as ties: that gap is mostly \emph{shared} across the three agents --- on s5e10 a common shift of $0.00025$ against mutual scatter of only $0.00002$ --- so the paired construction subtracts it out.

\paragraph{The correct construction.} Measure how much the \emph{gap between two agents} moves across the two subsets --- the common drift cancels in the subtraction. Writing $d_{\mathrm{pub}}$ and $d_{\mathrm{priv}}$ for the between-agent score difference on the public and private subsets, a verdict requires both:
\begin{align}
\operatorname{sign}(d_{\mathrm{priv}}) &= \operatorname{sign}(d_{\mathrm{pub}})
  && \text{(the ordering replicates on disjoint samples)} \label{eq:sign} \\
|d_{\mathrm{priv}}| &> |d_{\mathrm{priv}} - d_{\mathrm{pub}}|
  && \text{(the gap exceeds its own movement)} \label{eq:gap}
\end{align}

\paragraph{Statistical properties of the decision rule.} Condition~\eqref{eq:gap} is algebraically equivalent to $d_{\mathrm{pub}}\,(2d_{\mathrm{priv}} - d_{\mathrm{pub}}) > 0$ and therefore \emph{implies} condition~\eqref{eq:sign}; both are stated because they diagnose different failures. The rule is a conservative screen rather than a calibrated hypothesis test --- under a null of true equivalence it still returns spurious verdicts at a computable, non-negligible rate --- and it adjudicates every leaderboard verdict in this report, including the benchmark duels of Section~\ref{sec:winrates}.


\paragraph{The second error term: between-run agent variance.} The paired test covers test-set
sampling noise only; agent execution is itself stochastic. On the four competitions with the
tightest development-lane duels, my-agent ran twice fresh under identical conditions and AIDE's
pinned re-run pairs against its frozen baseline. Private-leaderboard spreads:

\subsection{Execution and Audit}
\label{sec:rerun}

The queue ran under operator approval and finished with all 20 lanes complete and audit-adjudicated, halting three times on the way --- twice on the same environment fault, once on its two-consecutive-audit-failures rule. That fault was ours rather than the method's: the sandbox bound the session's credential file read-only, so a lane whose access token expired mid-run could not write the refreshed token back and died; the post-benchmark fix binds it writable and refuses to start a lane whose token cannot outlive it. The audit gate was exercised in both directions, most sharply on \textbf{s3e19}, whose first attempt opened the raw knowledge library four times and was discarded. (It also tripped the network detector, but a re-scan with the corrected detector below finds no network command --- the hit was a Python variable named \texttt{nc} in a diagnostic heredoc, and the four raw opens carry the discard on their own.) The clean re-run scores \emph{worse} on local CV --- pooled OOF SMAPE 6.706 against the tainted attempt's 5.740 --- consistent with the contamination advantage being real and the audit pricing it correctly; a benchmark that discards a better-looking number and keeps the honest one has evidence its audit does something. The remaining incidents turned on transcript ordering: on s5e10, tps-aug-2022 and tps-sep-2022 the flagged raw-library opens all fell strictly \emph{after} the submission had been written and validated, so those scores stand (s5e10's hand-edit of the shared library after finalisation was reverted before any later lane ran). Exactly one instance crossed lanes: tps-jan-2022 started two minutes after aug-2022's write-back and its Stage 0.5 read served the polluted entries, so the operator killed that lane at one minute, reverted the library, and relaunched it clean --- the re-run raised the same class of blocker once more, again only after its own submission was validated, and was accepted on the same ordering argument.

The recurrence is the finding, not the individual incidents. Four of the twenty lanes (s5e10, tps-aug-2022, tps-sep-2022, tps-jan-2022) wrote their own \texttt{library\_writeback.py}, because the skill instructs every lane to write validated insights back through library tools this run root does not ship, and the root bound the shared library writable --- a design fault of the harness rather than of any lane, and only one instance of it crossed lanes. The fix mounts \texttt{knowledge/} read-only over the writable run root, so prevention no longer depends on after-the-fact detection. Re-reading the transcripts also exposed two defects in the auditor itself: its network detector matched the substring \texttt{kaggle} inside the path of the skill's own submission helper (two lane audits blocked on that string alone, each costing a manual scan and a recorded waiver) and, more seriously, its exemption list dropped every command beginning with \texttt{uv} --- which is every command a lane issues --- so \texttt{uv run kaggle competitions leaderboard}, the exact command the detector exists to catch, was never examined. Both are fixed; re-running the corrected auditor over all 20 transcripts returns zero network findings and leaves the genuine library blockers unchanged. A detector blind at its own target is the kind of defect only a post-hoc re-read finds.

\subsection{The Standing}
\label{sec:winrates}

The benchmark's headline reading is the full duel matrix: 20 competitions $\times$ 3 pairings $=$ 60 duels, each adjudicated by the rule of Section~\ref{sec:exp2} plus the per-competition variance floors. With all 20 lanes scored, every one of the 60 duels is adjudicated: 38 produce a verdict and 22 are undecidable --- the class the error model exists to catch, including a live replication of the development-lane s5e1 case (the public half of that test split favours NVIDIA, the private half favours my-agent, so the sign test cannot resolve it) and three duels killed by the variance floors --- two of them wins my-agent would otherwise have claimed (s3e16, and s5e10, where the private gap of $0.00003$ exactly equals AIDE's measured spread --- the case Section~\ref{sec:exp2} flagged in advance; the rule requires the gap to \emph{exceed} the floor, and we compare in exact decimal, so this duel stays undecidable rather than becoming a 17th my-agent win) and one a would-be AIDE win (s6e2, gap $0.00011$ under AIDE's $0.00020$ floor). Table~\ref{tab:winrates} gives the standing. my-agent leads outright: it wins 16 of its 24 decided duels ($66.7\%$; each yardstick wins 11 of its 26, $42.3\%$ --- the denominators differ because a duel is decided or not independently for each pair), takes the best private score on 10 of the 20 competitions (NVIDIA 7, AIDE 3), and its rerun submission beats its own development-lane private score on 13 of 20. Its sharpest losses fall where the mechanism of Section~\ref{sec:reversal} predicts they should: on tps-sep-2022, the one benchmark competition with a mature, high-quality public baseline, both yardsticks beat it decisively (5.48 and 9.72 SMAPE against 28.50), and on tps-jan-2022 NVIDIA's reproduction of the known GDP-ratio solution beats it 4.64 to 6.30. Reproduction dominates exactly where a strong public solution already exists; search wins the rest.

\section{The Injection Layer Under Control}

\subsection{Does the Dossier Fire on the Right Tasks?}
\label{sec:exp5v5}

\paragraph{Design and result.} Run on the 18 baseline competitions from the problem statement, data schema, and task priors alone --- minutes per competition, no training --- the dossier layer should fire only where external data is genuinely needed. It fires on exactly the two country-panel time-series competitions, s3e19 and jan-2022, whose accepted solutions are known to require external covariates, and reports ``no / unlikely'' on the other 16, which span iid-tabular, categorical, spectral and structured-output tasks. One caveat keeps this honest: the task priors were distilled from those two competitions' own failure analysis, so the silence is the informative half.

\subsection{What the Layer Delivers}
\label{sec:exp6v5}

\paragraph{Design.} Across the firing class --- s3e19, the held-out tps-sep-2022 and the pre-registered s5e1 --- matched arms differ only in the operator's added columns (verified digit-identical otherwise); every arm is submitted for real public and private scores and judged by the paired test of Section~\ref{sec:exp2}, and the s5e1 test was pre-registered. Full arm tables are in the companion case-studies document.

\paragraph{Effect.} Across the three competitions, \textbf{all six external-data arms beat
their no-external baselines, every one paired-significant}. On the canonical failure itself the
ablation against a searched champion from the identical pipeline with the layer disabled passes
the paired test, private $48.243$ against $50.455$ --- both controlled arms, not the benchmark
s3e19 lane, which scored $49.40878$ (Table~\ref{tab:bench}).

\paragraph{The operating rule the experiments imposed.} One negative result ships with the
layer: no local signal we tested selects which \emph{form} the injection should take.
Cross-validation, a held-out-year protocol, and a proportionality diagnostic each misrank the
form verdict (0-for-3); the diagnostic --- the one selector that survived hindsight --- was
put to a pre-registered test on s5e1, its prediction committed to version control before any
model was fitted, and its named falsifier fired. The layer therefore \emph{races both forms
inside the search, always}, and the one surviving post-hoc candidate (horizon length) is
committed as the H-HORIZON pre-registration for the next firing-class competition
(Section~\ref{sec:future}).

\paragraph{What the judgment layer proposes, and what actually consumes it.} The layer's coverage
ledger overstated itself: operators were emitted into a key no search driver read while the
dispatcher recorded them as realized. A produced artifact with no consumer is indistinguishable
from a working one in every log, score table and coverage metric --- inside the very mechanism
built to prevent silent truncation (Appendix~\ref{app:coverage}). \textbf{An architecture claim
needs a consumer trace, not a vocabulary count.}

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Contributions}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Placement against two frozen, methodologically distinct yardsticks, under a rule
  committed before the scores existed.} All 20 lanes audited and scored on the real leaderboard:
  16 wins to 8 losses, $66.7\%$ of my-agent's decided duels against $42.3\%$ for each yardstick
  (Section~\ref{sec:winrates}), plus the three-way best on all three mid-complexity competitions.

  \item \textbf{An end-to-end agent whose knowledge enters at problem identification, not at
  blending} (Sections~\ref{sec:v5} and~\ref{sec:exp5v5}--\ref{sec:exp6v5}): the dossier fires
  only where external data is genuinely needed, every controlled external-data arm beat its
  matched baseline, and --- the negative result --- no local signal selects the injection's
  \emph{form}, the one selector surviving hindsight killed by its own pre-registered falsifier.

  \item \textbf{A ranking is a property of method $\times$ evaluation set}, demonstrated rather
  than asserted and applied to our own agent first (Section~\ref{sec:reversal}).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{A Ranking Belongs to Method $\times$ Evaluation Set}
\label{sec:reversal}

The most important methodological lesson of the development runs: swap the competition set and the paired-test winner flipped between a Playground-only subset and the broader benchmark set (those standings belong to superseded development lanes and are not reprinted here; the mechanism is a property of the methods and survives). Reproduction dominates where a mature public solution exists, autonomous methods dominate on obscure, build-your-own-pipeline tasks, and relative verdicts binarize many near-tie gaps, so the paired win rate is highly sensitive to sample composition even while absolute position (median PR) stays stable. The s5e1 duel of Section~\ref{sec:winrates} reproduces the caution inside one competition: the public and private halves of a single test split order two agents oppositely.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{The significance test covers only test-set sampling noise}, not between-run agent variance. That variance is measured for my-agent and AIDE on four competitions (Section~\ref{sec:exp2}) and enters the error model as a per-competition floor; NVIDIA's term remains unmeasured.
  \item \textbf{The duels are not independent trials.} Each competition contributes three duels from six shared scores, so the effective sample size is closer to 20 competitions than to 60 duels. And reproduced kernels were tuned by their authors against the realized public leaderboard, so exchangeability is weakest exactly where the reproduction method is strongest.
  \item \textbf{Budgets are per-method conventions, not compute-matched}: AIDE's default 20 steps, my-agent's 37--224 evaluated nodes plus a linear pass, NVIDIA's minutes of reproduction --- each method ``as shipped.''
  \item \textbf{The sample is small, sensitive to its composition, and partly added post hoc.} Removing one competition moves a win rate by several points --- dropping s3e19 alone takes my-agent from $66.7\%$ to $72.7\%$; dropping s3e3 takes NVIDIA from $42.3\%$ to $37.5\%$ (Section~\ref{sec:reversal}). The two firing-class competitions joined after their leaderboards had been consulted for the injection experiments --- disclosed, not hidden.
  \item \textbf{The experience-library asymmetry is disclosed, not eliminated}: my-agent carries cross-competition memory; the other two cold-start.
  \item \textbf{The mid-complexity lanes ran without the injection layer and without tree search} (Section~\ref{sec:exp7run2}), and offline grading yields one score per submission, outside the paired test's reach.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Open Problems}
\label{sec:future}

\paragraph{Operator form cannot be selected from any local signal we have tested --- 0-for-3, with
one selector killed by pre-registration.} The one surviving candidate, horizon length, is post-hoc;
it is committed as the H-HORIZON pre-registration, to be tested on the next firing-class
competition before its leaderboard is consulted. This is the sharpest open question here.

\paragraph{The firing class is small}, so the controlled evidence rests on two clean test points
plus one held-out replication; extending it is worth more than re-running the competitions where
the layer stays silent, which changes nothing the layer does.

\paragraph{The experience library's residual weak link is retrieval quality.} Enforcement is
closed --- an experiment record without a \texttt{library\_query} trace is invalid, checked
mechanically by the audit tool. Quality is not: a neighbouring entry over-claimed and had to be
corrected against leaderboard evidence, and no mechanism yet scores retrieved priors for
applicability. Smaller items: whitelisting further source classes (prior-competition archives,
public text corpora), and a direct ablation of the experience library.

\section{Conclusion}

The benchmark verdict is complete: all 20 transcript-audited lanes were scored on the real leaderboard and adjudicated by the error model committed in advance (Section~\ref{sec:apparatus}) --- my-agent leads with 16 wins to 8 losses, $66.7\%$ of its decided duels, while 22 of the 60 duels stay undecidable at the measured noise scale. The architectural answer is placement: downstream injection measured near-null, so knowledge enters at problem identification, and the layer's controlled, leaderboard-scored evidence carries the claim (Section~\ref{sec:exp6v5}). Two negative results ship with it and count as the method working: no local signal selects the injection's form, the one selector surviving hindsight killed by its own pre-registered falsifier; and the audit discarded a contaminated lane whose tainted score looked better than its clean replacement's. A benchmark that can kill its own hypotheses is the thing this study set out to build.

\subsection{Execution Environment}

All lanes ran on ARM64 Linux (Ubuntu 24.04) with an NVIDIA GB10 GPU, under a uv-managed Python 3.13 environment whose versions are frozen in \texttt{uv.lock}.
